Tidal energy is an attractive energy source because the tidal cycles are predictable, making energy forecasting robust. The tidal energy potential in the United States (US) is greater than any other country in the world, with theoretical estimates of 400 TWh/year (Coles et al. (2025)). However, tidal energy harvesters have not been adopted by utility-scale actors because predicting power generation and turbine loading in confined flow channels is fundamentally different than rotors, turbines, and propellers in unconfined flows, such as in the atmosphere. 

The legacy of rotor modeling in confined flows, or blockage, is to relate the performance of rotors in a confined channel with an equivalent unblocked rotor through an adjustment in velocity. A multitude of such "blockage models" have been proposed in literature, but often successfully predict the power and thrust of a rotor in a narrow range of conditions (Ross and Polagye (2020)). A key deficiency is the lack of high-quality validation data of rotors in confined flows. While experimental measurements have been conducted, scaling turbines to the appropriate Reynolds Numbers, Froude numbers, and tip-speed ratios is challenging in the experimental setup due to size constraints and cavitation effects. However, direct numerical simulation of all relevant scales, from blade boundary-layer turbulence to array-scale wake dynamics, remains intractable without novel computing strategies. Tidal channels compound this challenge by compressing an enormous range of dynamically relevant scales into shallow geometries where they are not cleanly separated: confined wakes interact with channel boundaries and neighboring turbines within tens of diameters, creating inter-scale feedback that undermines the decoupled modeling assumptions of existing engineering tools. 

Confinement is simultaneously the greatest advantage and the greatest modeling challenge of tidal energy: channel boundaries impede bypass flow, raising the extractable power limit well beyond the open-flow Betz limit — theoretically to $C_{P,\text{max}} = \frac{16}{27}(1-B)^{-2}$ (Garrett & Cummins, 2007), where $B$ is the channel blockage ratio — but the accompanying amplification of thrust loading and modification of near-wake structure depend on blockage in ways that actuator disk models calibrated for unconfined flow cannot reliably capture.

To resolve the blade-level hydrodynamics of flow confinement, this proposal will perform simulations using the ExaWind stack (Sprague et al. (2020), Sharma et al. (2024)): a massively parallel, multifidelity numerical flow solver developed by the National Lab of the Rockies (NLR). Blade-level flow physics are resolved through overset meshes using Reynolds-Averaged Navier Stokes (RANS) simulations, which couple with structural solvers like OpenFAST to provide an accurate solid-body geometry. The RANS simulation results are nested within a turbulence-resolving large eddy simulation to provide feedback of the rotor- and array-level effects down to the blade scale. These next-generation simulations will reveal hydrodynamics critical to the operation and optimization of tidal energy harvesters, and will provide the physics-informed closure models needed to bridge the gap between blade-scale flow physics and array-scale engineering design.
